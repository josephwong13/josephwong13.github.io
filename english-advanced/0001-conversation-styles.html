Lesson 1 of 5 · Series: Conversational Language

Conversation Styles

Three people walk into the same meeting.

Elena sits down, nods at everyone, and says one sentence. The room listens. She doesn't add another word unless asked. Later, someone says: "Elena's feedback was worth the whole hour."

Ravi answers every question with context, background, and a detour. He covers every angle — and then some. When he finally finishes, people have forgotten what they asked.

Clara says what everyone else is thinking but won't say. The uncomfortable truth. The budget numbers nobody wants to look at. She doesn't raise her voice or apologise — she just names it.

Three people. Three ways of speaking. None wrong — but each lands completely differently.

📖 Words

Word 1 of 3
laconic
/ləˈkɒn.ɪk/ · lak-ON-ik · adjective · 簡潔的，言簡意賅的
Using very few words — deliberately. A laconic person isn't shy; they choose brevity as a tool. It conveys weight, reserve, or mild disapproval.
↳ Back to the story: Elena was laconic — one sentence that carried more weight than anyone else's presentation.
📰 News:The general's laconic briefing — "We advance at dawn" — said everything that needed saying.
💼 Work:His laconic email reply was three words: "I disagree. Why?"
📚 Literature:Hemingway is famous for his laconic prose — short sentences, immense weight.
Word 2 of 3
verbose
/vɜːˈbəʊs/ · ver-BOHSS · adjective · 冗長的，囉嗦的
Using more words than needed. Suggests inefficiency or lack of clarity — can be neutral (descriptive) or mildly critical depending on tone.
↳ Back to the story: Ravi was verbose — by the time he finished his answer, nobody remembered the question.
📧 Email:Her verbose email took three paragraphs to ask a single yes-or-no question.
💼 Work:The boss is known for verbose presentations that could be five slides but somehow become thirty.
📚 Academic:The paper was so verbose that its core finding was buried in the footnotes.
Word 3 of 3
candid
/ˈkæn.dɪd/ · KAN-did · adjective · 坦率的，直言不諱的
Honest and direct, especially when the truth is uncomfortable. Unlike "blunt" (which can be insensitive), candid suggests sincerity without cruelty.
↳ Back to the story: Clara was candid — she named the budget problem nobody wanted to mention.
💼 Work:In the performance review, she was candid about needing to improve her time management.
🏠 Personal:I'll be candid — I don't think this plan is going to work the way you described it.
📰 Media:The CEO gave a candid interview about the company's struggles, admitting past mistakes.

💬 Phrases

Phrase 1 of 2
cut to the chase
Get to the point. Skip the pleasantries, context, and buildup — say what matters directly.
Origin: Film editing jargon — directors would "cut to the chase" instead of building up to it through exposition. · 開門見山，直奔主題
↳ Back to the story: Everyone wished Ravi would cut to the chase instead of giving them the full backstory.
💼 Work:Let me cut to the chase — we're being acquired. The paperwork starts Monday.
🎬 Movies:The film could've cut to the chase instead of spending twenty minutes on backstory.
Phrase 2 of 2
not mince words
To speak directly and plainly, without softening the message. Almost always used in the negative form — "doesn't mince words" means they say exactly what they mean, even if it's harsh.
("Mince" here means to chop into small pieces — so "not mincing" means not breaking your words into gentle pieces.) · 直言不諱，毫不婉轉
↳ Back to the story: Clara doesn't mince words — she told the room the project was failing in four seconds flat.
💼 Work:The reviewer didn't mince words: "This code is unmaintainable and needs a full rewrite."
🏠 Personal:To not mince words, I think you're making a huge mistake — and I say that because I care.
