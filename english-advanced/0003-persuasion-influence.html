Lesson 3 of 5 · Series: Conversational Language

Persuasion & Influence

Three startups. Three pitches. One room of investors.

Anika opened with a single chart. It showed a problem everybody recognised — and her solution's growth curve going straight up. She didn't beg. She showed the numbers and let them speak. By the third slide, people were nodding.

Marcus walked in and immediately admitted: "Our first prototype failed. It was terrible." He smiled. The investors laughed. Then he told them what they learned — and why this version worked. The room softened. They trusted him.

Susan kept saying how much she respected everyone's time — then spent twenty minutes on vague projections. When asked about revenue, she smiled: "Well, that depends on how you define revenue." The investors exchanged glances.

Anika convinced with evidence. Marcus won trust by being open. Susan lost them by pretending.

📖 Words

Word 1 of 3
compelling
/kəmˈpɛl.ɪŋ/ · kəm-PEL-ing · adjective · 令人信服的，有說服力的
Very convincing — so strong that it makes you believe or agree. A compelling argument doesn't just sound good; it leaves you with no easy way to disagree.
↳ Back to the story: Anika's pitch was compelling — one chart, clear data, and the investors were already nodding.
⚖️ Law:The lawyer presented compelling evidence that changed the jury's mind in ten minutes.
📊 Data:The report makes a compelling case for switching to renewable energy — the numbers speak for themselves.
📖 Personal:Her compelling story about growing up in a small town made everyone at the conference stop and listen.
Word 2 of 3
disarming
/dɪsˈɑː.mɪŋ/ · diss-AR-ming · adjective · 使人消除戒心的，讓人放鬆的
Making you feel less angry, suspicious, or defensive — usually through honesty, warmth, or unexpected humility. A disarming person makes you drop your guard.
↳ Back to the story: Marcus was disarming — by admitting failure upfront, he made the investors trust him.
🎙️ Interview:His disarming honesty in the job interview — "I actually know very little about that system" — made the hiring manager trust him more.
🤝 Negotiation:She started the negotiation with a disarming smile and said, "I think we both know this first offer isn't realistic."
💬 Apology:His disarming apology — "You're right, I messed up, and here's how I'll fix it" — ended the argument instantly.
Word 3 of 3
disingenuous
/ˌdɪs.ɪnˈdʒɛn.ju.əs/ · dis-in-JEN-yoo-əs · adjective · 不真誠的，虛偽的
Not sincere — pretending to know less or care more than you really do. A disingenuous person says the right words but you sense they don't mean them.
↳ Back to the story: Susan was disingenuous — "it depends on how you define revenue" sounded clever but fooled nobody.
🗳️ Politics:The politician gave a disingenuous answer — he talked around the question for three minutes without saying anything.
🏢 Corporate:The company's "we care deeply about our employees" statement felt disingenuous after the layoffs.
🙅 Excuses:His disingenuous excuse — "I didn't get your email" — was obvious since we'd discussed it in person twice.

💬 Phrases

Phrase 1 of 2
talk into
To persuade someone to do something they were initially reluctant to do.
Structure: talk + [someone] + into + [something/doing something]
· 說服某人做某事
↳ Back to the story: Anika talked the investors into taking a meeting, even though they were initially sceptical of early-stage startups.
👫 Personal:My friends talked me into going skydiving — I was terrified, but I don't regret it.
💼 Work:The team talked the manager into extending the deadline by showing the quality impact of rushing.
Phrase 2 of 2
win over
To gain someone's support, approval, or favour through effort and persuasion — especially when they were initially against you.
Structure: win + [someone] + over / win over + [someone]
· 爭取到支持，說服
↳ Back to the story: Marcus won over the investors by being honest about his failures instead of hiding them.
🎤 Audience:The comedian won over a hostile crowd within five minutes by making fun of himself first.
💼 Sales:It took three meetings, but she finally won over the difficult client with patience and results.
