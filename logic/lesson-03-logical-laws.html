Logical Laws

Scenario: A philosopher asserts: "It cannot both be raining and not raining at the same time and place." Another replies: "That's just one of your arbitrary rules — I believe both can be true." Why is this second reply incoherent — and what logical law makes it so?

The Law of Non-Contradiction

LAW [IFL2 Ch4]

Law of Non-Contradiction (LNC)

For any proposition P: it is impossible for both P and ¬P to be true simultaneously.

Symbolically: ¬(P ∧ ¬P)

LNC is not a deduction from further premises — it is a foundational constraint on coherent thought. Any system that rejects LNC collapses into trivialism: everything is true and false simultaneously.

⚠ LNC IS NOT ABOUT TRUTH VALUES

LNC vs "a proposition can't be both true and false"

LNC governs contradictory propositions within the same system of thought — not the meta-level fact that a proposition can be true at one time and false at another (that is a different, valid situation). "It is raining" at t₁ and "it is not raining" at t₂ are not contradictory — they concern different times.

The Law of Excluded Middle

LAW [IFL2 Ch4]

Law of Excluded Middle (LEM)

For any proposition P: either P is true, or ¬P is true — there is no third option.

Symbolically: P ∨ ¬P

LEM does not say we always know which — it says there is no indeterminate middle truth value between truth and falsity. LEM is rejected by intuitionist logics, which hold that proving P and proving ¬P require different kinds of evidence.

LNC and LEM together

LNC and LEM are independent laws — rejecting one does not force you to reject the other. However, classical logic accepts both, and they jointly imply that every proposition is either true or false (but not both, not neither).

WORKED EXAMPLE

Applying LNC

Claim: "Descartes is mortal and Descartes is not mortal are both true."

Analysis: This violates LNC. The conjunction P ∧ ¬P is a contradiction — no possible situation makes both conjuncts true. Any argument premising this claim is formally inconsistent.
