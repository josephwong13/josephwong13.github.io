Logical Form

Scenario: Two arguments: (A) "All ravens are black. This bird is a raven. Therefore, this bird is black." (B) "All conspirators are liars. Jones is a conspirator. Therefore, Jones is a liar." Different subject matter, different conclusions — yet both share the same inferential skeleton. What is that skeleton, and why does sharing it mean both arguments stand or fall together?

What is logical form?

Logical form is what remains when you strip away all content-specific vocabulary — names, properties, relations — and replace them with placeholder variables. The result is a schematic pattern that reveals the structural relationships between premises and conclusion.

KEY INSIGHT [IFL2 Ch6]

Same form = same logical fate

Two arguments with the same logical form are either both valid or both invalid. This is the foundational principle that makes formal logic possible — form, not content, determines validity.

Abstraction in practice

WORKED EXAMPLE

From natural language to form

Original: All ravens are black. This bird is a raven. Therefore, this bird is black.

Abstract: All A are B. x is an A. Therefore, x is a B.

Every instance of this form — regardless of what A, B, and x stand for — is valid. The form is the syllogism Barbara, one of the most fundamental valid forms in classical logic.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Substitution instance

Form: All A are B. x is A. Therefore, x is B.

Instance: All politicians are corrupt. Obama is a politician. Therefore, Obama is corrupt.

If the form is valid, this instance is valid — regardless of whether "Obama is corrupt" is actually true. Validity concerns the inference, not the premise truth.

Why form is invisible in natural language

Natural language obscures form through rhetorical elaboration, lexical ambiguity, and contextual assumptions. Formalisation — the process of reconstructing an argument's logical form — is a deliberate act of translation. The same argument can often be formalised in multiple ways, some more revealing than others.

⚠ FORMALISATION IS INTERPRETIVE

There is no mechanical procedure

Deciding what counts as the logical form of a natural-language argument requires judgement. The same sentence can be formalised as a universal quantifier, an existential quantifier, or a simple atomic proposition — depending on the level of analysis appropriate to the context.
