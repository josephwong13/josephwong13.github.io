1. Words you'll need

Chapter 2 asked whether any state can be justified. Chapter 3 accepts that one can, and asks the next question: which state, run by whom?

Definitions

Democracy — rule by the people. The definition is contested: who counts as “the people”, and what does “rule” mean?

Majority rule — decisions follow whichever side has more votes.

Tyranny of the majority — a majority using its numbers to override the interests or rights of everyone else.

General will — Rousseau's idea of what the people would will if they considered only the common good, as opposed to the sum of private preferences.

Representation — citizens authorise others to decide on their behalf.

2. Everyone says democracy — nobody says why

Almost nobody defends monarchy or aristocracy today. Democracy is treated as the only fully justifiable form of government — and yet most people, asked why, produce something circular: because the people should decide.

Wolff's move is to press on that. What is democracy? Is it simply counting heads? And if so, why should a head count be authoritative about anything?

3. Two ideas pulling apart

Two commitments inside democratic theory pull in opposite directions.

The tension

Majority rule says decisions should follow the greater number. Consideration for individuals says each person's interests count, and must not simply be overridden. Take the first seriously and 51% can do what they like to 49%. Take the second seriously and you need limits on what majorities may decide — which is no longer pure majority rule.

Every real democracy is a compromise between these. The philosophical problem is that the compromise has never been given a clean justification.

4. Plato and the craft analogy

Plato · Athens · c. 375 BC · Republic, Books VI–VII

Ruling is a skill, not a vote

Plato's attack is simple and unsettling. In every other domain, we want people who know what they are doing. We don't vote on how to treat a disease, or on whether the ship should steer toward the rocks.

The craft analogy
  1. Governing is a skill (technē), with genuine expertise and real stakes.
  2. Where a skill exists, we should be guided by those who have it — not by whoever can shout loudest.
  3. Most people have no expertise in governing.
  4. Therefore, letting everyone decide is like letting passengers steer the ship.

Conclusion: democracy is rule by the ignorant, and it flatters them by calling that freedom.

The ship image is the one to remember: a captain who is chosen by the crew and flatters them will wreck the ship, while the true navigator — who knows the stars — is dismissed as useless.

5. Plato's guardianship

The constructive half of Plato's argument is that the state should be run by guardians: a class selected and educated from childhood for the task, living without private property, and forbidden from using office for personal gain.

Two things usually get missed.

What Plato is not saying

He is not defending inherited aristocracy. His guardians are selected by ability and trained hard — in principle, anyone could be chosen. And his worry is not that the masses are wicked, but that they are being flattered by people who want power.

The trouble is obvious too: who guards the guardians? Plato's answer — an elaborate education and the abolition of private interest — demands a level of institutional faith few readers share.

6. Rousseau and the general will

Rousseau · Geneva · The Social Contract (1762)

The people as a single body

Rousseau offers a third option, neither head-counting nor expertise. Sovereignty belongs to the people as a body, and that body has a general will: what it would will if it attended to the common good rather than to private advantage.

This is not the same as the sum of everyone's preferences. It is what remains when private interest is set aside. And here is the disturbing part — Rousseau says that anyone who refuses the general will can be “forced to be free”.

The danger

If someone else can decide what you really will, your actual objections stop counting. Rousseau's general will has been read as the ancestor of both participatory democracy and totalitarian politics — and both readings have evidence.

7. Mill and representation

Mill · England · Considerations on Representative Government (1861)

Participation as education

Mill takes the modern route: representative government. Citizens do not decide laws; they choose people to decide them.

His case is partly about competence, but mainly about character. Taking part in public decisions — jury service, local office, voting — educates people out of narrow self-interest. Democracy is justified not because the people always decide well, but because deciding is how they become capable of deciding.

He also worried openly about competence, once suggesting extra votes for the educated. That proposal is a useful reminder that Mill's liberalism was not egalitarian in the way modern readers expect.

8. The hard objection

Grant Plato his craft analogy. Does guardianship follow?

The reply that keeps democracy alive

Governing involves an irreducibly political question that no expertise settles: whose interests count, and how are conflicting interests to be weighed? A doctor can tell you what will cure you. She cannot tell you whether you should be made to pay for someone else's cure. That is not a technical question — so it cannot be delegated to technicians.

Notice what this does not prove. It does not show that ordinary people decide well, only that the decision is legitimately theirs. That is weaker than most defences of democracy, and more honest.

10. Common misconceptions

Misconception 1

“Democracy means the majority decides everything.” — Then there is nothing a majority may not do to a minority. Every defensible democracy constrains majorities, which means it is not pure majority rule.

Misconception 2

“Plato wanted kings.” — He wanted trained guardians selected for ability, with private interest abolished. That is a meritocracy of a very specific, and very demanding, kind.

Misconception 3

“Rousseau's general will is just the majority vote.” — If it were, he would not have needed the concept. It is meant to be what the people would will apart from private interest — which is precisely what makes it dangerous.

11. Glossary

Craft analogy
Plato's argument that governing is a skill, so it should be practised by those with expertise.
Guardianship
Rule by a trained class selected for ability and denied private property.
General will
Rousseau's conception of the common good as willed by the people as a body.
Representative government
A system in which citizens choose decision-makers rather than deciding directly.
Deliberative democracy
The view that legitimacy comes from reasoned public deliberation, not merely from counting votes.
Tyranny of the majority
The domination of minorities by a numerical majority.

1. Big words, made small

Plain words

Democracy — a system where the people decide, or choose who decides.

Majority — more than half.

Guardian — someone trained and trusted to look after the country.

Representative — someone you pick to decide for you.

2. Everyone says democracy

Almost everyone today says democracy is the best system.

But if you ask why, you often get: “because the people should decide.”

That isn't really an answer. It just says it again.

So let's ask properly: what is democracy? Is it just counting votes? And why should the bigger number win?

3. Two ideas that fight

Two things in democracy disagree with each other.

The fight

Idea 1: the side with more votes wins.

Idea 2: every person matters, and can't just be overruled.

If 51 people want to take everything from 49 people, idea 1 says they can. Idea 2 says they can't. You can't always have both.

4. Plato: would you vote on your surgery?

Plato · ancient Greece · about 375 BC

Some things need experts

Plato asked a simple question.

If you were sick, would you take a vote on which operation to have? Would you let the loudest person in the room decide?

No. You'd find a doctor.

Plato said running a country is like that. It's a skill. There are people who know how to do it, and people who don't.

So letting everyone vote is like letting the passengers steer the ship.

5. Plato: let the experts rule

Plato's answer: pick a group of guardians.

They would be chosen for how good they are, not because of who their parents were. They would be trained from childhood. And they would not be allowed to get rich from the job.

That way they'd care about the country, not themselves.

The big problem

Who watches the guardians? Who decides if they're doing a good job? If they're the experts, they might say “we are.” And then nobody can stop them.

6. Rousseau: what the people really want

Jean-Jacques Rousseau · 1712–1778

The general will

Rousseau said: the people should rule — but not just by counting votes.

He said there is something called the general will. It's what the people would want if they stopped thinking about themselves and thought about everyone.

That sounds nice. But here's the scary part.

Rousseau said people who refuse the general will can be “forced to be free.”

Think about that. If someone else decides what you really want, then what you actually say doesn't matter anymore.

7. Mill: we vote for people, not laws

John Stuart Mill · 1806–1873

Voting teaches people

Mill had a different answer. He said: we shouldn't vote on every law. We should vote for people who make the laws.

Why? Because taking part changes you.

When you help decide something real — a jury, a local meeting, an election — you stop only thinking about yourself. You learn to think about other people.

So democracy isn't good because people always choose well. It's good because choosing makes people better at choosing.

8. Does democracy survive the attack?

Plato's argument is strong. But there is a reply.

The reply

A doctor can tell you what will make you healthy. But a doctor can't tell you whether you should pay for someone else's medicine.

That isn't a medical question. It's a question about who matters and how we share — and no expert can answer it for us.

So the choice belongs to the people — even if the people sometimes choose badly.
