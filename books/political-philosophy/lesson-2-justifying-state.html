Justifying the State

We treat the state's authority as obvious. But you never agreed to it, and no one asked. Four attempts have been made to show you are bound anyway — and each one leaves something out.

1. Words you'll need

Chapter 1 asked whether we could live without a state. This chapter assumes we need one, and asks a sharper question: what gives this state the right to command me?

Definitions

Political obligation — a citizen's moral duty to obey the state. Not fear of punishment: a duty.

Consent — agreement given, in the paradigm case, explicitly and knowingly.

Tacit consent — agreement inferred from what you don't do: staying, not leaving, accepting benefits.

Legitimacy — whether the state's claim to authority is justified. Distinct from whether you personally owe it obedience.

Philosophical anarchism — the view that no state is legitimate, held by people who nonetheless obey the law and pay their taxes.

2. The question: you never signed anything

You did not agree to be governed. You were born here, registered, schooled, and now taxed. If you stop paying, eventually men with guns will come — legally, politely, but armed.

What makes that different from a protection racket? The answer most people reach for is: the government has the right. This chapter asks what could make that true.

Why it matters practically

If no answer works, it does not follow that we should abolish the state. It follows that a great deal of what states do rests on something other than the consent of the governed — and we should know what.

3. What would count as a justification?

Wolff starts by clarifying the target. A state is not just whoever has the guns; it claims a monopoly of legitimate force over a territory, and it claims authority over everyone inside it — citizens and visitors alike.

So a justification has to show something strong: that there are universal political obligations. Not that most people ought to obey, or that obedience is usually wise, but that everyone subject to the state is bound.

A distinction to hold onto

Justifying having a state is easier than justifying this state. You might prove that some authority is necessary and still fail to show that the particular one you live under has a claim on you.

4. Consent theory

Locke · Second Treatise, ch. VIII

Obligation must be self-assumed

Locke's starting point is that we are naturally free. No one is born a subject. So the only thing that could place you under a government is your own agreement:

The consent argument
  1. Humans are by nature free and equal, subject to no political power.
  2. The only way to become legitimately subject to a government is to consent to it.
  3. Therefore, political obligation rests on consent — and nothing else.

Upshot: no consent, no obligation, however beneficial the state may be.

The attraction is obvious: it makes authority depend on you. The difficulty is equally obvious. Almost nobody has consented. You didn't sign at birth, and you have no memory of agreeing.

5. Tacit consent, and Hume's objection

Locke sees the problem and offers a patch. Consent need not be spoken. If you remain in a territory, enjoy its protection, and could in principle leave, your staying counts as agreement — tacit consent.

David Hume found this absurd, and his objections are still the ones to beat.

Hume's objections
  1. Staying isn't choosing. A poor labourer cannot emigrate; leaving means abandoning family, work, language and everything he owns. A choice with only ruinous options isn't a choice.
  2. Birth isn't consent. We are born into a government as we are born into a language. Nobody thinks speaking English means agreeing to English politics.
  3. The sailor. Hume's image: a man who stays on a leaking ship because the shore is hostile has not agreed to the voyage.

Upshot: tacit consent explains obedience, not obligation.

Note what Hume is doing. He is not denying that we often obey. He is denying that obedience is evidence of agreement — which is exactly what consent theory needs it to be.

6. Utilitarianism

The utilitarian sidesteps consent entirely. The test of the state is not whether you agreed, but whether it produces better consequences than the alternatives.

On this view, the state is justified because life under it is safer, richer, and freer than life without it — for everyone counted equally. The fact that you never consented is irrelevant, in the same way that you needn't consent to a vaccination programme that saves your life.

The standard objection

If consequences alone justify coercion, then sacrificing an innocent person for the greater good is justified whenever the sums favour it. Most people think that is a reductio: it shows the theory permits what no legitimate state may do.

7. The principle of fairness

A fourth route, associated with H. L. A. Hart and Rawls: obligation arises from fair play. If you accept the benefits of a cooperative scheme, you owe your share of the burden. You didn't ask for the road network, but you use it.

The fairness argument
  1. A just scheme of cooperation produces benefits for those who take part.
  2. If you voluntarily accept those benefits, you acquire an obligation to do your part.
  3. The state is such a scheme.

Upshot: you owe obedience because you have been taking, not because you agreed.

Nozick's reply is the sharpest: benefits alone don't oblige you. If a radio station broadcasts into your flat, you don't owe it money for the music you can't easily avoid. Acceptance must be voluntary — and with public goods like defence and law, it never is.

8. The hard objection

Put the four side by side and a pattern appears: each theory identifies something real — agreement, benefit, consequences — and then overreaches in claiming it generates an obligation for everyone.

This is the position A. John Simmons calls philosophical anarchism. It is not a call to throw stones. It says: no existing state is legitimate in the strong sense, because no general obligation has been established — and yet we may still have reasons to obey particular laws, and states may still be justified in doing many things.

The distinction that saves the chapter

Legitimacy is about whether the state may coerce. Obligation is about whether you must comply, and why. A state can be legitimate without you owing it a general duty of obedience — and that gap is where most of modern political philosophy lives.

10. Common misconceptions

Misconception 1

“Consent just means voting.” — Voting might be evidence of participation, but it is not an explicit agreement to obey. Many who vote would refuse if asked directly.

Misconception 2

“Utilitarianism doesn't care about individuals.” — It counts each person's welfare equally. The objection is not that it ignores people, but that it aggregates them.

Misconception 3

“If no theory works, no state is justified.” — That confuses obligation with legitimacy. You can conclude that no general duty of obedience exists without concluding that all coercion is wrong.

11. Glossary

Political obligation
A moral duty to obey the state, as opposed to merely being forced to.
Express consent
Explicit, stated agreement — swearing an oath, signing a form.
Tacit consent
Agreement inferred from behaviour, especially from remaining in a territory.
Principle of fairness
The claim that accepting benefits from a just cooperative scheme creates an obligation to contribute.
Philosophical anarchism
The view that no state is legitimate, combined with ordinary law-abiding behaviour.
Legitimacy
Whether a state may rightfully coerce — distinct from whether you owe it obedience.

1. Big words, made small

Plain words

Obey — doing what someone tells you, because they told you.

Duty — something you should do, even if nobody is watching.

Consent — saying yes to something.

Tacit consent — a fancy way of saying “you said yes without saying anything” — like staying quiet.

2. You never signed anything

Here is a strange thing.

Nobody ever asked you if you wanted a government. You were just born here.

Now the government takes some of your money every month. If you refuse, they can punish you.

If a stranger did that, we would call it stealing. So why is it okay when the government does it?

Most people say: “Because the government has the right.”

Okay. But where does that right come from?

3. What even is a state?

A state is not just “whoever has the most guns.”

It is the group that says: only we are allowed to use force here. Police, courts, prisons, borders. And it says this about everyone in the country — not just people who agreed.

So if you want to say a government is fair, you have to show something big: that everyone in the country should obey it.

That is a lot to prove.

4. Locke: you said yes

John Locke · England · 1632–1704

You must have agreed

Locke said: people are born free. Nobody is born a servant.

So the only way a government can have power over you is if you agreed to it.

That sounds fair. But it has a big problem.

Did you agree? Do you remember signing anything? Did anyone ask?

Almost nobody has. So by Locke's own rule, almost nobody has to obey.

5. Hume: but did you really?

David Hume · Scotland · 1711–1776

Staying is not the same as saying yes

Locke tried to fix the problem. He said: if you stay in the country, that counts as agreeing. You could leave, and you didn't. So you said yes without speaking.

Hume said that is silly.

Imagine a poor farmer. To leave the country, he would have to leave his family, his job, his language, and everything he owns. That is not a real choice.

Hume's picture: a sailor stays on a leaking ship because the shore is dangerous. Did he agree to the voyage? Of course not.

You also didn't choose your language. Nobody thinks speaking English means you agreed to English politics.

6. The greatest good for the most people

Some thinkers skip the question of agreement completely.

They say: don't ask whether people agreed. Ask whether the government makes life better.

Is life safer with a government? Richer? Less violent? If yes, that's the justification. Everyone counts equally, and everyone is better off.

That sounds reasonable. But there is a problem.

The problem

If “more good overall” is all that matters, then hurting one innocent person to help many would be fine. Most of us think that's wrong. So something is missing.

7. You took the benefits

Here is another idea. It goes like this.

You use the roads. You use the hospitals. You are protected by the police and the army. You didn't pay for all of that yourself.

So you owe something back. Not because you agreed — because you took.

It is like a group project at school. You didn't say you'd join. But you used the work everyone else did. Now it's your turn to do your part.

The problem

What if you didn't ask for the benefit? If a radio station plays music into your house, do you owe them money? Probably not. For “you took it” to work, you have to have taken it on purpose.

8. So why does it still feel wrong?

Look at the four ideas together.

Each one gets something right. People do agree sometimes. Benefits do matter. Consequences do matter.

But none of them proves that everyone must obey.

Some philosophers accept this. They are called philosophical anarchists. That sounds scary, but it isn't. They don't want to smash anything. They just say: no government has fully proved that it has the right to command you.

One important difference

There are two separate questions. May the state make me? And must I obey? They are not the same. A state can be allowed to do things without you personally owing it obedience.
