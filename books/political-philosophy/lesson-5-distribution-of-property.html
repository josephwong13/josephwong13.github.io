1. Words you'll need

Chapter 4 protected what you may do. This chapter asks what you may own — and whether the answer changes if someone else has almost nothing.

Definitions

Distributive justice — the question of how goods, income and opportunities ought to be shared.

Proviso — Locke's condition that acquiring property is legitimate only if enough and as good is left for others.

Veil of ignorance — Rawls's device: choose principles of justice without knowing your place in society.

Difference principle — inequalities are justified only if they benefit the least advantaged.

Entitlement — Nozick's idea that a holding is just if it was justly acquired and justly transferred, whatever pattern results.

2. The parade of incomes

Imagine everyone in the country walking past you in a line, ordered from poorest to richest. Their height shows their income: average income is average height.

For most of the parade you see dwarfs — people far below average. Near the end come the tall, and at the very back a few giants whose height exceeds the horizon.

Why the image works

It replaces an abstraction (“inequality”) with something you can see. And it forces a question no statistic does: is this arrangement just?

3. Liberty against equality

Two things we value collide here.

Liberty says people may acquire, trade and keep what they get. Equality says the resulting gap may be indefensible regardless of how it arose.

Every position in this chapter is a different way of weighing those two.

4. Locke: you made it, you own it

Locke · Second Treatise, ch. V

Property begins with labour

Locke's argument is that the world starts as common property, and private property arises when someone mixes their labour with it. Pick apples in an unowned field and the apples are yours — you did the work.

The labour argument
  1. The earth is given to humanity in common.
  2. Mixing your labour with something makes it yours.
  3. So acquisition is justified by work, not by permission or agreement.

But there is a limit: you may take only what you can use before it spoils, and only where “enough and as good” is left for others.

That proviso is where the argument strains. Once money exists, goods no longer spoil, and land can be enclosed indefinitely. Locke's limits look designed for a world before markets — and he knows it.

5. The free market

The market position says: if every transfer was voluntary and no one was defrauded, the resulting distribution is just, however unequal it looks.

Redistribution, on this view, is not generosity — it is taking something from someone who owns it, to give it to someone who does not, on the basis of a pattern the taker prefers.

The strongest version

It does not claim the outcome is beautiful. It claims the process was legitimate, and that legitimate processes generate legitimate results.

6. Rawls and the veil of ignorance

John Rawls · A Theory of Justice (1971)

Choose the rules without knowing your place

Rawls asks you to imagine choosing the basic rules of society from behind a veil of ignorance. You do not know whether you will be born rich or poor, clever or not, healthy or disabled.

Behind that veil, what would you choose? Not a system that maximises the average — you might be below it. Not a system that forbids all inequality — you might be among the winners.

Rawls's two principles

1. Each person has an equal claim to basic liberties.

2. Social and economic inequalities are justified only if they are attached to positions open to all, and they benefit the least advantaged members of society.

The second clause is the difference principle. It does not demand equality. It demands that any inequality make the worst-off better than they would be under equality — otherwise it is unjust.

7. Nozick's objection

Robert Nozick · Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974)

Justice is historical, not a pattern

Nozick's objection is structural. Rawls's principle is patterned: it says what the distribution should look like now. But patterns cannot survive liberty.

The Wilt Chamberlain argument
  1. Start with whatever distribution you think is just — including a perfectly equal one.
  2. People voluntarily pay to watch Chamberlain play. Each payment is fair.
  3. After the season, he is vastly richer, and the pattern is broken.
  4. To restore the pattern, you must forbid or reverse voluntary transfers.

Conclusion: maintaining a pattern requires continuous interference with liberty.

Nozick's alternative is historical: a holding is just if it was justly acquired (original acquisition), justly transferred (free exchange), or justly rectified if the first two failed. No pattern required.

8. Can any distribution be just?

The unresolved quarrel

Both sides have a real point. Nozick is right that patterns require interference with voluntary action. Rawls is right that a process can be voluntary at every step and still produce a distribution no one would have agreed to in advance — especially if the starting point was unjust.

The heart of it is historical: who owned what at the beginning, and how did they get it? Nozick's theory is only as good as his account of original acquisition — which is Locke's, proviso and all.

10. Common misconceptions

Misconception 1

“Rawls wanted equality.” — He wanted the least advantaged to be as well off as possible. That can require large inequalities, if they raise the floor.

Misconception 2

“Nozick defends the rich.” — He defends entitlement, not wealth. A distribution he would reject can still be very unequal, and one he would accept can be flat.

Misconception 3

“Locke's labour argument settles it.” — It rests entirely on the proviso, and the proviso may be impossible to meet once land is scarce and money circulates.

1. Big words, made small

Plain words

Own — it's yours, and other people can't just take it.

Fair share — how much each person should get.

Inequality — when some people have much more than others.

Tax — money the government takes to pay for things everyone uses.

2. The parade of incomes

Imagine everyone in the country walks past you in a line.

They go from the poorest to the richest. And their height shows how much money they have.

Most people would be very short. Near the end, people get tall. And at the very back, a few would be giants — taller than the buildings.

That's what money looks like when you draw it.

Now the question: is that fair?

3. Freedom vs fairness

Two ideas pull against each other here.

The fight

Freedom: people should keep what they earn. If you build something and sell it, the money is yours.

Fairness: it doesn't matter how you got it — if others have almost nothing, something is wrong.

4. Locke: you made it, you own it

John Locke · 1632–1704

Work makes it yours

Locke said: the world belongs to everyone at the start.

But if you pick apples from a wild tree, the apples are yours. Why? Because you did the work. You mixed your effort into them.

So owning things comes from working, not from asking permission.

But there's a catch

Locke added a rule: you can only take what you can use, and you must leave enough and as good for other people.

That rule is hard to keep once land runs out — and once money exists, nothing ever goes bad, so people can keep collecting.

5. The market knows best

Some people say: if nobody cheated, and every deal was fair, then the result is fair too.

Even if some people end up with a lot and others with very little.

Because nobody took anything. Everything was bought, sold or given, freely.

So taking money from the rich to help the poor isn't kindness — it's taking something that belongs to someone else.

6. Rawls: design it without knowing your place

John Rawls · 1921–2002

The veil of ignorance

Rawls asks you to imagine something.

You have to choose the rules of society. But you don't know who you'll be. You don't know if you'll be born rich or poor, healthy or sick, clever or not.

What rules would you pick?

You wouldn't pick “the richest people get everything” — because you might be poor.

You wouldn't pick “everyone gets exactly the same” either — because a bit of inequality can make everyone better off.

Rawls's answer

Inequality is okay only if it helps the worst-off people.

If the poorest person is better off with the gap than without it, the gap is allowed.

7. Nozick: fairness has no history

Robert Nozick · 1938–2002

It depends how you got it

Nozick said Rawls is making a mistake.

Rawls looks at the shape of the money — who has how much right now. Nozick says that's not the question.

The question is: how did they get it?

His example: start with everyone equal. Then people choose to pay to watch a great basketball player. Nobody is forced. After a year, he's rich.

The shape changed. But every single payment was fair.

So Nozick says: to keep things “even”, you'd have to stop people giving their money to whoever they want.

8. Can any distribution be just?

The problem nobody has solved

Nozick is right that forcing a shape means interfering with free choices.

Rawls is right that a result can come from fair steps and still be one nobody would agree to in advance.

And there is a deeper question underneath both: who owned what at the very beginning, and how did they get it? If the start was unfair — land taken, people enslaved — then everything built on it is questionable.
