1. Words you'll need

Chapter 6a asked who was missing inside the state. This chapter asks whether the state's edge should matter at all.

Definitions

Global justice — whether principles of justice apply between societies, not only within them.

Cosmopolitanism — the view that every person counts equally, regardless of nationality.

Statism — the view that obligations to compatriots are genuinely stronger than obligations to outsiders.

Intergenerational justice — what we owe to people who will exist later.

Discounting — weighing future benefits less than present ones.

2. Why does a border change anything?

A child born in one country has a life expectancy, income and education that may be several times worse than a child born a few hundred miles away.

Nothing about the children differs except where a line was drawn. If justice is about what people deserve, the line looks arbitrary.

The uncomfortable comparison

Inside a state, we think it obvious that a rich person owes something to a poor compatriot through tax. Across a border, the same gap is called foreign aid — optional, discretionary, and a fraction of the size.

3. The assumption nobody noticed

Rawls is explicit that his theory is for a “closed society”: people enter by birth and leave by death. The original position asks what citizens would choose.

That is a powerful simplification — and it hides a decision. Why should the unit of justice be the state? If the veil of ignorance is meant to stop you knowing your advantages, why does it stop at the border and let you know your nationality?

Rawls's later answer, in The Law of Peoples, is that liberal peoples owe one another duties, but not the same difference principle globally. Critics — Beitz, Pogge — say the veil should have been global.

4. The drowning child

Peter Singer · “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” (1972)

Proximity is not a moral fact

Singer's example is deliberately simple. You are walking past a shallow pond. A child is drowning. You can wade in and save them; you will ruin your shoes.

Nobody thinks you may keep walking. And yet — if distance and nationality are morally irrelevant, the same reasoning reaches people dying of poverty abroad.

Singer's principle
  1. If you can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable importance, you ought to do it.
  2. Distance and nationality do not change what is very bad, or what you are able to prevent.
  3. Therefore you ought to give substantially — not just generously.

Upshot: the ordinary distinction between duty and charity collapses.

The objection

If the principle is taken literally, almost everything you spend on yourself is unjustified — and obligations become limitless. Most philosophers accept the direction of the argument while rejecting its full demands, which leaves the boundary unexplained.

5. Who has the right to come in?

If all persons are morally equal, what justifies stopping someone at a border?

Two sides of the border
Open bordersThe right to exclude
Freedom of movement is a basic liberty; restricting it needs justification like any other restrictionCommunities have a right to self-determination, and a community is partly constituted by its members
Birthplace is morally arbitrary — the accident of where you were born should not fix your life chancesStates carry obligations to their own citizens first, including welfare and culture
Restriction is normally justified by harm — but the harm to the excluded is enormousUncontrolled entry can undermine the institutions that make justice possible

Notice that the open-borders case is not an argument for no rules. It is an argument that the burden of proof sits with the border, and that the usual defences do not meet it.

6. People who do not exist yet

Climate policy forces a question that earlier chapters never faced: what do we owe people who are not born?

Three complications make this genuinely hard.

Reciprocity fails. Justice between contemporaries can be reciprocal. Future people can give us nothing.

Discounting. Economists weigh future benefits less. But a discount rate applied to lives is not obviously a technical choice — it is a decision about how much a distant person's welfare counts.

The non-identity problem. The people who will exist depend on what we do now. If a different policy means different people are born, it is hard to say those people were harmed — they would not exist otherwise.

Why it matters politically

Future people cannot vote, protest, or sue. They have no standing in the institutions that decide their conditions — which is precisely why the obligation has to be argued rather than assumed.

7. Can one theory cover everyone?

The deep worry

Every theory in this book was built for a bounded community: compatriots who can coerce each other, share institutions, and reciprocate. Stretch it globally and the machinery — consent, reciprocity, mutual coercion — stops working. Stretch it across time and it fails almost completely.

One response is that this shows the theories were parochial. Another is that it shows justice is parochial: perhaps the strong demands only apply among people who share a political system, and beyond that we have duties of humanity, not justice.

9. Common misconceptions

Misconception 1

“Global justice means foreign aid.” — Aid is charity; the argument is about entitlement and obligation, which is a much stronger claim.

Misconception 2

“Open borders means no borders.” — It means the burden of proof shifts: restrictions must be justified, not assumed.

Misconception 3

“Discounting the future is just economics.” — Choosing a discount rate is choosing how much a future life counts. That is a moral decision wearing a technical costume.

1. Big words, made small

Plain words

Border — the line between two countries.

Fair — treating people as they deserve, not by accident of birth.

Future people — people who will be born later. They don't exist yet.

2. Why does a border matter?

Think about two children.

One is born here. One is born a few hundred miles away, in a poorer country.

They didn't choose where to be born. Nobody does.

But one will live much longer, go to a better school, and have more choices — just because of a line on a map.

Is that fair? Most people feel something is wrong. But it's hard to say exactly what.

3. The thing nobody noticed

Rawls (from chapter 5) asked us to choose fair rules without knowing who we'd be.

But there's something odd.

He said you don't know if you're rich or poor. But you do know which country you're in.

Why? If the whole point is not knowing your advantages — why are you allowed to know your nationality?

That's the hole this chapter looks at.

4. The drowning child

Peter Singer · 1972

Would you walk past?

You're walking past a shallow pond. A child is drowning.

You could wade in and save them. It would ruin your shoes.

Would you keep walking?

Of course not. Nobody would.

Now Singer's point.

The argument

If you must save the child, it's because you can — and the cost to you is small.

But there are children dying of poverty far away, right now. You can help them too. The cost is small.

The only difference is distance. And distance isn't a moral reason.

5. Who gets to come in?

If everyone matters equally, why can a country stop people at the border?

Two sides
Open borders say…Countries say…
Where you're born shouldn't decide your lifeA country is a community, and communities choose their members
Moving freely is a basic freedomA country must look after its own people first
If you stop someone, you need a good reasonToo many people too fast can break things

Notice: nobody serious says “no rules at all.” The question is who has to prove their case.

6. People who aren't born yet

Some choices affect people who don't exist yet. Climate change is the big one.

What do we owe them? Three problems:

They can't pay us back. Normal fairness works both ways. This can't.

We count them as less. People often say a life in 100 years matters less than a life now. But why?

They might not exist. If we do things differently, different people will be born. So it's hard to say we “harmed” anyone.

The simple version

People who don't exist yet can't vote, can't protest, and can't sue. So nobody speaks for them — unless we choose to.

7. Can one idea cover everyone?

The problem

All the ideas in this book were built for one country — people who live together, obey the same laws, and can pay each other back.

Stretch that to the whole world and it gets thin. Stretch it to the future and it almost disappears.

So maybe the honest answer is: some duties are for people in your own country, and beyond that we owe something weaker — not nothing, but not the same.
