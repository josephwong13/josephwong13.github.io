1. Words you'll need

Every chapter so far quietly assumed a citizen: an adult, able-bodied, usually male, and living inside one country. This chapter asks what that assumption cost.

Definitions

Oversight — Wolff's term for a blind spot: not a refuted argument, but a question never asked.

Domestic justice — justice within a single state, as opposed to across borders.

Formal equality — the same rules applied to everyone on paper.

Structural injustice — disadvantage produced by how institutions are arranged, without anyone intending it.

Public/private divide — the assumption that the household is outside politics and therefore outside justice.

2. Who did the great thinkers forget?

Hobbes's state of nature is populated by heads of households. Locke's property owner is someone who works land. Rawls's chooser behind the veil is an adult with a life plan.

Each is a specific kind of person. The theories are not obviously wrong about that person — they are silent about everyone else.

The pattern

The excluded are not random. They are the people whose labour, care or dependence made the theorist's independent citizen possible in the first place.

3. What an “oversight” is

Wolff names three thinkers as having committed oversights: Marx, Wollstonecraft and Mill.

That list is deliberately odd. Wollstonecraft and Mill are included not because they ignored injustice, but because each saw one axis clearly and another not at all. Marx saw class and treated other divisions as secondary. Mill defended liberty while excluding those he considered uncivilised.

An oversight, then, is not stupidity. It is what happens when a theory is built around the problems its author could see.

4. Gender and the private sphere

Mary Wollstonecraft · A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792)

The personal is political

Wollstonecraft's target is not the law but the household. Women were excluded from education, property and the vote — and the exclusion was defended as natural, domestic and therefore outside politics.

Her argument is that this is circular: you deny someone education, then cite their lack of education as proof they should not be trusted with decisions.

The move that mattered

If the household is where power is exercised — who eats last, who leaves school, who is safe — then calling it “private” does not remove it from justice. It removes it from scrutiny.

Later feminists compressed this into a slogan: the personal is political. It is a claim about scope, not about banning family life.

5. Race: from formal to structural

Formal equality is easy to state: the same rules for everyone, regardless of race. Most legal systems now claim it.

The difficulty is that identical rules applied to unequal starting points reproduce the inequality. If your grandparents could not own property, a neutral inheritance law is not neutral in effect.

The distinction that matters

Formal: no rule mentions race. Structural: the outcome still tracks race, because history is carried in wealth, housing, schooling and policing.

This is why “I don't see race” is not a neutral position. It describes the rules while ignoring the starting line.

6. Disability and sexual orientation

Both cases expose a hidden assumption in liberal theory: that justice means treating like people alike.

For disability, the social model argues the disadvantage lies less in bodies than in environments — stairs instead of ramps, exams that measure speed, workplaces built for one kind of body. Treating everyone identically preserves the barrier.

For sexual orientation, the issue is not only legal permission but the baseline against which “normal” is measured. Formal neutrality can leave a majority's way of life as the default, with everyone else defined by departure from it.

Why this troubles the earlier chapters

Chapter 4's harm principle protects you from interference. But it says little about being able to participate. Liberty and capability are not the same thing.

7. Is adding groups enough?

The objection

The obvious fix is to add the missing groups to the theory. But that may not work. If the framework was built around an independent adult with no caring responsibilities, adding women to it does not address the fact that the framework assumes someone else is doing the caring — usually a woman.

The deeper challenge is that these are not separate problems bolted onto a working theory. They question the picture of society the theory started from: a collection of independent individuals rather than people who depend on each other.

9. Common misconceptions

Misconception 1

“An oversight means the thinker was wrong.” — It means a question went unasked. Much of Hobbes, Locke and Rawls survives; what changes is the scope of the question.

Misconception 2

“Formal equality ends injustice.” — It removes explicit discrimination from the rulebook while leaving inherited advantage intact.

Misconception 3

“Adding women and minorities fixes the theory.” — Only if the theory's basic picture of a self-sufficient adult was right to begin with. That picture is exactly what is in dispute.

10. Glossary

Oversight
A question a theory never asks, as distinct from an error it argues for.
Domestic justice
Justice as it operates inside a single state.
Structural injustice
Disadvantage produced by institutional arrangements rather than individual intent.
Social model of disability
The view that disability arises from barriers in society, not only from bodies.
Public/private divide
The assumption that households are outside the scope of political justice.

1. Big words, made small

Plain words

Oversight — something you didn't notice. Not a lie, just a blind spot.

Formal equality — the same rule for everyone, on paper.

Structural — built into how things are set up, not into one person's choice.

2. Who did the great thinkers forget?

Look closely at the earlier chapters.

Hobbes imagines a man with a house. Locke imagines someone who owns land. Rawls imagines an adult with a plan for their life.

Notice who is missing. Almost every example is a grown man who doesn't have to look after anyone.

Where are the women? The children? The people who need care? The people whose country was taken?

3. Three famous blind spots

Wolff names three thinkers who had blind spots: Marx, Wollstonecraft and Mill.

That's a strange list — because two of them fought for justice.

But that's the point. You can see one unfairness very clearly and miss another completely.

4. What about women?

Mary Wollstonecraft · 1759–1797

The home is political too

For a long time, women could not vote, own property, or go to university.

People said: that's fine, because a woman's place is the home. And the home is private — not politics.

Wollstonecraft said that's a trick.

If power is used inside the home — who eats last, who leaves school, who is safe — then calling it “private” doesn't make it fair. It just means nobody checks.

Later, people summed this up: the personal is political.

5. What about race?

Today most laws say: the same rules for everyone, whatever your race.

That sounds fair. But it isn't the whole story.

A simple example

Two runners start a race. One starts at the line. The other starts 100 metres back.

Now give them exactly the same rules. Is the race fair?

Same rules on paper can still produce very different results — because people start in different places.

6. What about disability?

Here's something easy to miss.

If a building has stairs and no ramp, who is stopped? Not everyone.

The problem isn't only the person's body. It's the building.

Same with a test that measures speed, or a job that assumes you can stand all day. The rules are the same for everyone — and that's exactly what keeps some people out.

7. Is adding people enough?

The problem

Easy fix: just add women and other missing people to the theory.

But the theory was built around a person with no one to look after. Add women to it and you still haven't asked: who does the caring?

The real question is bigger. These thinkers imagined society as a bunch of independent adults. But nobody is independent. We all start as children, and most of us need help at some point.
