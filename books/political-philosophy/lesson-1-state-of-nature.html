The State of Nature

We obey governments every day — we pay, register, comply, queue. This chapter asks the question underneath all of that: what would we lose if there were no government at all? Four philosophers gave four very different answers, and none of them agrees.

1. Five words to fix first

Nothing later will make sense if these slide around, so here they are up front. You don't need to memorise them — just let them settle.

Definitions

State — an institution that claims a monopoly on legitimate force over a territory. Police, courts, tax offices, borders. Crucially, it doesn't merely have power; it claims the right to use it.

Authority — the right to command, as opposed to the power to compel. A gunman has power over you. He has no authority.

Legitimacy — whether the state's claim to authority is justified, not merely effective.

Political obligation — the duty of a citizen to obey. The question of this whole book: do we actually have one, and if so, why?

Coercion — being made to act by threat. If you don't pay tax, eventually someone takes your money by force. That is the fact political philosophy has to justify.

Hold onto the authority / power distinction. Almost every mistake in this subject comes from sliding between “the state can make me” and “the state may make me.”

2. The puzzle: authority without a question

Imagine a stranger instructs you to hand over a third of your income, or he will lock you in a room. We have a word for that: robbery, or extortion. Now substitute the state, and the same transaction becomes taxation, and refusing it becomes tax evasion.

What changed? Not the coercion. What changed is that the state claims authority — and most of us accept the claim without ever asking what makes it true. We are born into a system already running, and we absorb its legitimacy the way we absorb a language.

The question

Political philosophy begins by refusing to take that for granted: what, if anything, justifies one institution holding a monopoly on force over everyone inside a border?

The state of nature is the tool philosophers invented to answer that question honestly. Strip the state away and ask what remains.

3. Why philosophers imagine away the state

This is the part beginners most often get wrong, so it's worth being blunt: the state of nature is not a claim about the past. Nobody thinks there was a morning when humans woke up without government and signed a contract.

Think of how an engineer tests a bridge. She doesn't need to know how the bridge was built; she asks what happens if you remove a support. If the bridge stands, the support was decorative. If it collapses, the support was doing work — and now you know what work.

The device

The state of nature removes one support — political institutions — and asks what collapses. Whatever collapses is what the state is for. Whatever survives didn't need justifying.

This is why the four thinkers below disagree so violently while using the same tool: they disagree about what would collapse. For Hobbes, everything. For Rousseau, only something we'd be better off without.

4. Aristotle — the state is natural

384–322 BC · Athens · Politics, Book I

We are political animals

Aristotle's answer is to refuse the experiment. You cannot remove the support, he thinks, because the building is the support. Humans are by nature political animals — creatures built for life in a polis, a city-state.

His argument is a comparison with other animals:

Aristotle's argument
  1. Every creature has a characteristic function, and its good consists in performing it well.
  2. Humans alone possess speech (logos), which lets us communicate not just pain and pleasure but justice and injustice — right and wrong.
  3. Therefore the human function is to live a shared life organised around those judgements.
  4. A polis is exactly that organised shared life.

Conclusion: the polis exists “by nature,” and a human outside one is incomplete — “either a beast or a god.”

Context you need

Aristotle isn't defending any government you'd recognise. He's writing about the Greek city-state — small enough to gather in one place — and he explicitly argues some people are “natural slaves.” The view is also hierarchical and exclusionary in ways he took for granted and we shouldn't. Read him as the strongest version of a position, not as a policy proposal.

Why it still matters: if Aristotle is right, the state of nature is not a risky place but an impossible one — and the burden falls entirely on anyone who thinks government is a human invention. Hobbes and Locke both accept that burden.

5. Hobbes — the war of all against all

1588–1679 · England · Leviathan, ch. XIII (1651)

Peace is a technology

Hobbes wrote during the English Civil War — a king beheaded, a country at war with itself, neighbours denouncing neighbours. Leviathan is a book written by someone watching what happens when authority collapses. That experience is the emotional engine of the argument.

His picture of human beings is deliberately unflattering, and deliberately mechanical: we are bodies in motion, forever pursuing what he calls felicity — the continual success in getting what we want.

The argument, step by step

Hobbes's argument
  1. Power is instrumental. “The power of a man… is his present means to obtain some future apparent good.” To be secure in getting what we want, we need power.
  2. We are roughly equal. Not in strength — in ability to threaten. The weakest can kill the strongest in his sleep, or with allies. Hobbes: “from equality of ability ariseth equality of hope in the attaining of our ends.”
  3. Resources are finite. Two people wanting the same thing become enemies, and each rationally seeks to pre-empt the other. This is competition.
  4. Uncertainty is intolerable. Even if no one attacks, not knowing whether they will is itself a threat. So we strike first to secure ourselves — diffidence. Add glory, the pleasure of being seen to win, and violence becomes self-sustaining.

Conclusion: without a common power to overawe them all, life is “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.”

The common misreading

Hobbes is not saying humans are evil. His claim is structural: even decent, cautious, reasonable people generate war, because the situation rewards pre-emption. He is describing a trap, not a character flaw.

Notice what the argument buys him. If this is right, any state — even a bad one — is better than none, because the alternative is worse than tyranny. That is a very strong conclusion, and much of the rest of the book is an argument about whether he's entitled to it.

6. Locke — liberty under law

1632–1704 · England · Two Treatises of Government (1689)

The same experiment, a different result

Locke runs the identical thought experiment and gets the opposite picture. His state of nature is not war. It is “a state of perfect freedom” — but freedom within a boundary, because a law of nature binds everyone: no one may harm another's “life, health, liberty, or possessions.”

These are the natural rights. And because the law of nature is real, everyone holds what Locke calls the executive power of that law: you may punish someone who violates it. Justice exists before any government does.

So what goes wrong?

Not human nature — procedure. Locke lists three “inconveniences”: there is no settled, known law; no impartial judge; and no power to enforce the judgement once made. So each person becomes judge in their own case, and disputes escalate.

Government, on this account, is a remedy for a technical defect. We don't hand over our rights; we appoint an arbiter. That is why Locke can be quoted on both sides of modern arguments — by liberals limiting government, and by revolutionaries justifying its overthrow when it betrays its trust.

7. Rousseau — society is the corruption

1712–1778 · Geneva · Discourse on Inequality (1755)

The experiment turned inside out

Rousseau accepts the method and inverts the conclusion. Compare a wild animal with a house pet, he suggests — and then ask which is happier. Savage humans, on his account, have few needs and the means to meet them; they feel pity for suffering and a modest desire for self-preservation. Crucially, they do not compare themselves to others.

Two words worth learning

Amour de soi — healthy self-love, the desire to preserve oneself. Presocial, harmless.

Amour-propre — vanity: the desire to be esteemed, to rank above others. This one only exists in society, and it is the engine of inequality, envy and domination.

So the state doesn't rescue us from savagery. It is the final stage of a fall that began when people started living together and asking who was better. Rousseau even allows a “nascent society” — the first villages — as the happiest and most stable moment in human history, precisely because it came before comparison hardened into hierarchy.

The common misreading

Rousseau is not saying “go back to the forest.” You can't, and he knows it. The Discourse is a diagnosis of what modern society does to us, not a travel plan. His remedy, in The Social Contract, is political: rebuild the state on a legitimate basis.

8. The anarchist challenge

Beginners often assume anarchism means chaos — no rules, everyone fending for themselves. That is a straw man. Philosophical anarchism is a claim about justification: the state's authority is illegitimate, and we can imagine orderly cooperation without it.

Now the crucial move. A rational anarchist need not deny a single Hobbesian risk. She can grant that life without a state is dangerous, that cooperation is hard, that violence is real — and still ask:

The residual question

Does the danger of anarchy justify this state — with these borders, this monopoly, this particular distribution of power over me — or only justify some coordinating institution? The Hobbesian argument may prove less than it appears to.

Wolff's verdict on the chapter, and the sentence to remember, is that once the state of nature is described carefully, the gap between rational anarchism and a defence of the state turns out to be vanishingly small. The chapter that looked like a proof of government ends as an open question.

10. Common misconceptions

Misconception 1

“The state of nature is what actually happened before governments.” — No. It's a device for testing justification; empirical history neither confirms nor refutes it.

Misconception 2

“Hobbes and Locke disagree about whether people are good.” — Only partly. Locke's state of nature is peaceful because of a binding natural law, not because people are nice; his problem is a missing judge, not a missing conscience.

Misconception 3

“If Hobbes is right, the argument for government is settled.” — That is exactly what Wolff denies. Grant every risk of anarchy and you still haven't justified a particular state's authority.

11. Glossary

State of nature
A thought experiment describing human life without political institutions. Used to test what the state contributes — not a claim about prehistory.
Political obligation
A citizen's duty to obey the state. The central question of the book.
Legitimacy
Whether the state's claim to authority is justified, as distinct from whether it is effective.
Natural law
Locke's idea that moral rules bind humans independently of any government or legislature.
Felicity
Hobbes's term for the continual, never-finished success in getting what one desires.
Amour-propre
Rousseau's term for vanity — the desire to be esteemed by others, which arises only in society.
Philosophical anarchism
The view that the state's authority is illegitimate, held without necessarily advocating violence or chaos.
Inconvenience (Locke)
A defect of the state of nature short of war: no settled law, no impartial judge, no enforcement.

1. Big words, made small

These four words come up a lot. Here is what they really mean.

Plain words

The state (or government) — the people who make the rules and can make you follow them. Police. Courts. Taxes.

Authority — having the right to tell someone what to do. Not just being strong enough to make them.

Obey — doing what someone tells you, because they told you.

Justify — to give a good reason why something is fair. Not “because I said so.”

2. The strange thing we all accept

Imagine a stranger comes up to you and says:

“Give me one third of your money, or I will lock you in a room.”

That is stealing. You would call the police.

Now imagine the government says it. We call that tax. And we call it normal.

Same action. Different name. Why?

Because we believe the government has the right to ask. Where does that belief come from? That is the question this whole chapter is about.

3. The game with no government

Philosophers like to play a game. They ask: what if there were no government at all?

Not really — nobody wants to try it. It is a “what if” question, like asking:

Think of it like this

If you cut one leg off a table, does it fall down? If it falls, that leg was holding something up. If it stays standing, the leg wasn't doing much.

Take the government away. What breaks? That tells us what government is for.

Careful — this is not history

Nobody is saying there was a real morning when people woke up with no government. It's an imagination game. It helps us test whether government is fair.

4. Aristotle: we need other people

384–322 BC · ancient Greece

People are like bees

Aristotle lived a very long time ago, in Greece. He looked at animals and noticed something.

Bees cannot live alone. One bee on its own dies. Bees need the hive.

He said humans are the same. We need other people. We need someone to talk to, someone to swap things with, someone to help us decide what is fair.

So he said: government is natural. It is not an invention. It is part of being human, like a hive is part of being a bee.

If he is right, then there was never a time without government — and nobody needs to prove it is a good idea.

5. Hobbes: everyone is scared

1588–1679 · England

The war of everyone against everyone

Hobbes lived in England while it was fighting a war with itself. He saw what happens when the rules stop working.

He asked: what if there were no police, no courts, no laws?

Now picture it.

You have food. Your neighbour wants food. You don't know if your neighbour will attack you tonight.

So what do you do? You attack first, to be safe.

But your neighbour is thinking the same thing. So they attack first too.

Now everyone is scared, and everyone is attacking. Nobody can relax. Nobody can grow food, or build, or sleep properly.

Hobbes said life like that would be “nasty, brutish and short.”

He is not saying people are evil

That's the part people get wrong. Hobbes thought normal, careful people would still end up fighting. The situation makes it happen, not the person.

So Hobbes said: we need one big power that frightens everyone into keeping the peace. Even a bad government is better than none — because no government is worse.

6. Locke: we need a referee

1632–1704 · England

Someone has to make the call

Locke read Hobbes and said: hold on. Life without government would not be a war.

He thought people are born with rights. You have the right to live. The right to be free. The right to keep your things.

And there is a natural rule, he said: don't hurt other people.

So the problem is not that people are bad. The problem is nobody can settle an argument.

Think of a football game

Imagine playing football with no referee. Two players crash into each other. One says “he pushed me!” The other says “no, he tripped me!”

Nobody can decide. So they argue, then they fight. The game stops.

You don't need nicer players. You need a referee.

That is what Locke thought government is for. Not to take your rights away — to be the referee.

And that is why he said: if a government stops doing its job, the people can replace it.

7. Rousseau: comparing made us jealous

1712–1778 · Geneva

Society is what spoiled us

Rousseau said something very different.

He thought early humans were fine. They had food. They had somewhere to sleep. They did not worry about what other people thought of them.

Then people started living together in villages. And they started comparing.

Am I richer than him? Am I funnier? Does everyone like me more?

Rousseau had a name for this: amour-propre. It means caring too much about what other people think.

Once people compare, they want more. They want to look better. That is where inequality starts.

So for Rousseau, government is not the rescue. It is the last step in a bad direction.

He is not saying “go live in the forest”

You can't, and he knew it. He was saying: our society makes us unhappy in a particular way — so let's build a better one.

8. The tricky question at the end

Here is the part that is easy to miss.

Suppose Hobbes is right. Suppose life with no government really is terrible.

Does that mean this government is okay? These borders? This leader? These rules?

Not necessarily.

It might only show that we need some kind of system. It does not show that this exact one is fair.

That is what Wolff means when he says the gap between “we don't need a government” and “this government is right” is very, very small.
