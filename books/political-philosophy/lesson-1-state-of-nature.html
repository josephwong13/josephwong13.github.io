The State of Nature

Two islands. In R. M. Ballantyne's The Coral Island (1858), three English boys are shipwrecked and live idyllically — they cooperate, defeat pirates, and thrive. In William Golding's Lord of the Flies (1954), boys land on a bountiful island and descend into tribal warfare. Same premise, opposite conclusions.

Wolff opens with these two books because they stage the question political philosophy cannot avoid: what would life be like without government?

Why imagine a world without the state?

METHOD [Wolff Ch1]

The state of nature is a test, not a history

We take government for granted — police, courts, tax offices, all claiming the right to command. The state of nature strips that away and asks what, if anything, we lose.

It is a thought experiment: a device for isolating what the state does for us, and therefore what could justify it.

Aristotle: the state is natural

POSITION 1 [Wolff Ch1]

We are political animals by nature

Aristotle holds the state exists by nature — humans are made for political life, as bees are made for hives. On this view a state of nature is not merely undesirable but impossible: we could not be human without it.

His test is stark: whoever can live outside a polis is "either a beast or a god." The burden then falls on anyone who thinks government is a human invention.

Hobbes: the war of all against all

POSITION 2 [Wolff Ch1 · Hobbes, Leviathan XIII]

Felicity, power, and fear

Hobbes begins from motion: we are material bodies forever pursuing felicity — continual success in getting what we desire. Since power is "present means to obtain some future apparent good," each of us must accumulate power to feel secure.

Add rough equality of ability, hence equality of hope, and three causes of quarrel — competition, diffidence, glory — and peace collapses. Even the strong must strike first, because others might.

Life without a common power becomes "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."

Locke: liberty under natural law

POSITION 3 [Wolff Ch1 · Locke, Two Treatises II]

Peaceable — but inconvenient

Locke's state of nature is not war. It is a state of liberty governed by a law of nature: no one may harm another's life, health, liberty, or possessions. Everyone holds executive power to punish transgressors.

The trouble is procedural. There is no settled, known law, no impartial judge, and no power to enforce judgement — so disputes escalate. Government is justified as the remedy for these inconveniences, not for total war.

Rousseau: society as the corruption

POSITION 4 [Wolff Ch1 · Rousseau, Discourse on Inequality]

The noble savage and amour-propre

Rousseau inverts the picture. Savage man has few needs and no vanity; pity and self-preservation suffice. What corrupts us is comparison — amour-propre, the desire to be esteemed by others, which only society creates.

So the state does not rescue us from savagery; it consummates a decline already begun. Hobbes, on this reading, described not nature but us — already spoiled by society.

The anarchist challenge

⚠ WOLFF'S STING [Wolff Ch1]

The gap is vanishingly small

A rational anarchist need not deny that life without a state is risky. She can concede every Hobbesian point and still ask: does that justify this state, with its monopoly, its borders, its coercive authority?

Wolff's unsettling conclusion: once the state of nature is described carefully, the distance between rational anarchism and a defence of the state shrinks almost to nothing. What looked like a knockdown argument for government becomes a question left open.

READ THIS CAREFULLY

What the device is not

It is not anthropology. No contractarian claims to know how humans once lived. The state of nature is a normative tool for testing justification — which is why empirical findings about early societies neither confirm nor refute it.

🔄 Takeaway. Four answers to one question: natural (Aristotle), unbearable (Hobbes), inconvenient (Locke), corrupting (Rousseau). Wolff's point is not that one wins, but that the winner is far less obvious than it looks — which sets up Lesson 2: what could actually justify a state?
