The Thought Cabinet

In Harry du Bois's head, the doors are open. All of them.

Electrochemistry whispers sweet poison in his ear. Rhetoric crafts elegant justifications for every failure. Inland Empire speaks in riddles about a world of hidden meaning. A dissonant chorus of voices — each one him, each one a stranger — argues over every move before he makes it.

He has become deeply introspective, but not by choice. Every conversation is a therapy session. Every doorway is a puzzle. He reaches for a bottle and must negotiate with himself: Volition says no, Electrochemistry says yes, Logic calculates the odds of being caught. The inner parliament never adjourns.

At his core, a single skill holds him upright: volition — the power of conscious will, the thing that says I choose even when everything inside him screams I can't. It is the last line between the man he could be and the man he was.

Kim Kitsuragi, his partner, watches the detective talk to himself. "You need to pull yourself together, Harry," he says, his voice flat and kind. And the voices do not stop — but the question they call into question is the deepest one: whether a shattered man can will himself whole again.
