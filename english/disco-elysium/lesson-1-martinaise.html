Martinaise

A man wakes up on a trashed hostel floor in a fishing village called Martinaise. He has no memory. His shirt is torn. His breath could strip paint.

He is utterly disheveled — three days of stubble, one shoe missing, a necktie that looks like it survived a war. The room around him mirrors his state: bottles everywhere, a broken lamp, the lingering scent of something spilled and regretted.

The thoughts come in fragments — a woman's face, a statuette from Kishaasi, the clatter of a ceiling fan — but nothing connects. He is delirious, half-convinced the body in the garden behind the hostel is somehow related to the storm of images in his skull. His own name is a mystery.

He has lived a dissolute life — that much is clear from the wreckage around him. A decade, maybe two, of bad decisions fossilised into muscle memory. The kind of life that leaves you waking up in places you don't recognise, next to people whose names you never learned.

He is at a loss — for words, for memories, for the basic architecture of who he is. But somewhere beneath the hangover, a detective's instinct stirs. He must wrestle with the fragments, piece together a self from the debris, and find out who — if anyone — is worth becoming.
