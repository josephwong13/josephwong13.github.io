Becoming Human

In the end, the mystery of the hanged man is solved. But Harry du Bois has been solving a much older mystery: who is he, and who can he become?

On the island, he faces Dora — the woman he loved and lost. It is not a reconciliation; it is a funeral for the man he used to be. And something breaks open inside him. Not a breakdown — a catharsis, a cleansing release that pours out of him in ugly, beautiful sobs on the cold sand.

He cannot undo what he did. The years of drinking, the cruelty, the self-destruction — these are carved into his history like bullet scars. But he can atone. Not through grand gestures, but through small, daily choices. To show up sober. To listen to Kim. To be kind to the children of Martinaise, even when they spit at him.

Something shifts. He becomes sovereign — not in the sense of power or dominion, but in the quiet sovereignty of a man who finally owns his own choices. The voices in his head do not go silent, but he learns to listen differently. He is the captain now. The voices are advisors, not commanders.

He must come to terms with the man he was — the failures, the betrayals, the years lost to the bottle. That man is not a stranger; he is himself, younger and sicker and more afraid. Harry holds his hand across time and says: I see you. I forgive you. Let's go.

He cannot exorcise his demons — that is not how demons work. But he can learn to sit with them, to understand them, to stop feeding them. And one morning, in the pale light of dawn over Revachol, he realises: that is enough.
