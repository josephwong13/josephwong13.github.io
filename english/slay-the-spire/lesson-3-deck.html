The Deck

After every battle, you're offered a choice: add one of three cards to your deck, or take none at all. This is where runs are won or lost.

Too many weak cards dilute your deck — you'll draw Strikes when you desperately need Blocks. The key is synergy: every card should work with the others. A card that applies Poison becomes devastating when paired with one that doubles Poison. This is how a deck transforms from random to unstoppable.

At the Merchant, you face a decisive moment. Do you buy that rare relic, or spend your gold removing a weak card? You must learn to weigh your options and sometimes cut your losses — selling a potion you'll never use to afford what you truly need.

📖 Words

Word 1 of 3
dilute
/daɪˈluːt/ · die-LOOT · verb / adjective · 稀釋；削弱
To make something weaker or less effective by adding other elements. A diluted deck has too many weak cards that reduce your chance of drawing the strong ones.
↳ From the story: Too many weak cards dilute your deck — you'll draw Strikes when you need Blocks.
💼 Work:Adding too many features will dilute the product's core value — users won't know what it's really for.
🍹 Cooking:If the soup is too salty, dilute it with water or add a peeled potato to absorb the excess.
📊 Finance:Issuing more shares will dilute the value of existing shareholders' stakes.
📝 Other forms & aspects
  • dilute (v., base form) — weaken by adding to it. Water will dilute the concentrate.
  • diluted (adj. / past participle) — already weakened by addition. The orange juice tasted watered down and diluted.
  • dilute (adj.) — also an adjective meaning weakened. A dilute acid is safer to handle.
  • dilution (n.) — the process of weakening; in finance, a reduction of share value. Share dilution is a concern for early investors.
  • dilute with + noun — common grammar: dilute with water, dilute with sugar. The chef diluted the sauce with stock.
  • diluted in + noun — used in chemistry. The acid was diluted in distilled water.
  • concentrated (adj., opposite) — made stronger, not weaker. Use concentrated juice for stronger flavor.
  • water down (phrasal verb, synonym) — weaken the impact of something. The report was watered down before publication.
Word 2 of 3
synergy
/ˈsɪn.ə.dʒi/ · SIN-ər-jee · noun · 協同作用；增效
The combined effect of elements working together that is greater than the sum of their individual effects.
↳ From the story: The key is synergy — every card should work with the others, creating effects far greater than any single card alone.
💼 Work:The synergy between the design and engineering teams produced a product neither could have built alone.
🎵 Music:The band's synergy on stage was electric — each musician feeding off the others' energy.
🤝 M&A:The merger was driven by expected synergies — combining the two sales forces would double market reach.
📝 Other forms & aspects
  • synergy (n., base form) — combined greater-than-sum effect. There's great synergy between sales and product.
  • synergies (n., plural) — multiple such combined effects; very common in business reports. The report identified three cost synergies from the merger.
  • synergistic (adj.) — producing such an effect. The two drugs have a synergistic effect when combined.
  • synergistically (adv.) — in a synergistic manner. The departments worked synergistically on the launch.
  • synergize (v., informal) — to produce synergy by working together. The two departments began to synergize on the launch.
  • synergy between A and B — common grammar pattern. There's real synergy between marketing and product.
  • in synergy — phrase used in technical writing. The two systems operate in synergy to balance the load.
  • corporate buzzword warning — note: synergy is heavily used in business jargon; in casual conversation a simpler word (teamwork, flow) often works better.
Word 3 of 3
decisive
/dɪˈsaɪ.sɪv/ · di-SYE-siv · adjective · 決定性的；果斷的
Settling an issue or producing a definite result; having the ability to make decisions quickly and effectively.
↳ From the story: At the Merchant, you face a decisive moment — one choice that will shape the rest of your run.
⚔️ History:The Battle of Midway was the decisive naval engagement that turned the war in the Pacific.
💼 Work:A good manager is decisive — they gather the facts, make a call, and take responsibility for the outcome.
🏟️ Sport:She scored the decisive goal in the 89th minute, securing her team's place in the final.
📝 Other forms & aspects
  • decisive (adj., base form) — producing a clear result, or quick to decide. His decisive strike won the fight.
  • decision (n.) — the choice that was made. The decision to delay launch was unanimous.
  • decisively (adv.) — in a way that produces a clear result. She voted decisively against the proposal.
  • decisiveness (n.) — the quality of being decisive. The CEO's decisiveness won the board's respect.
  • make / take a decision — common verb pattern. We need to make a decision by Friday.
  • decisive moment / factor / victory — common collocations. The decisive factor was price, not quality.
  • indecisive (adj., opposite) — unable to choose; producing no clear result. An indecisive leader loses the team's trust.
  • decisive vs. determined — note: decisive = settles an outcome or acts quickly; determined = committed to a goal. She was determined but not decisive.

💬 Phrases

Phrase 1 of 2
weigh your options
To carefully consider the advantages and disadvantages of each possible choice before making a decision.
· 權衡利弊；反覆斟酌
↳ From the story: You must learn to weigh your options — sometimes skipping a reward is the strongest play you can make.
🎓 Education:Before accepting any university offer, take time to weigh your options — consider location, cost, and course quality.
💼 Work:After receiving two job offers, she spent the weekend weighing her options before making a final decision.
📝 Other forms & aspects
  • weigh (one's) options — base form. Take a day to weigh your options.
  • weighed (one's) options (past) — already considered. She weighed her options carefully.
  • weighing (one's) options (continuous) — currently considering. We're still weighing our options before signing.
  • weigh up + noun — close variant common in British English. She weighed up the pros and cons.
  • weigh the pros and cons — common expansion. Let's weigh the pros and cons before committing.
  • weigh + noun — "weigh" can also be used with other objects: weigh risks, weigh consequences. The judge weighed the consequences carefully.
  • explore one's options — close synonym: to look into what's available. She's exploring her options before signing.
  • keep one's options open — related idiom: don't commit yet. I'm keeping my options open until next quarter.
Phrase 2 of 2
cut your losses
To abandon a failing plan or investment before the situation gets worse; to accept a small loss to avoid a bigger one.
· 及時止損
↳ From the story: Learn to cut your losses — selling a potion you'll never use to afford what you truly need.
📈 Finance:The investor decided to cut his losses and sell the stock before it dropped another 20%.
💼 Work:After six months on a failing project, management finally chose to cut their losses and redirect the team.
📝 Other forms & aspects
  • cut (one's) losses — base form. It's time to cut our losses and move on.
  • cut (one's) losses and run — extended form, emphasizing leaving the situation. They cut their losses and ran before the company collapsed.
  • cutting (one's) losses (continuous) — currently abandoning. We're cutting our losses on the failing product line.
  • cut + noun — note: cut also appears in cut costs, cut ties, cut corners (same root idea of trimming). They cut ties with the supplier after the scandal.
  • before the losses get worse — typical reasoning embedded in the phrase. Sell now, before the losses get worse.
  • cut one's losses and move on — extended idiom. After the breakup, she cut her losses and moved on.
  • throw good money after bad (related) — the OPPOSITE mistake: continuing to invest in a lost cause. Don't throw good money after bad — cut your losses now.
  • cut one's losses at + noun — common grammar: cut losses at $1000, at the first sign. Cut your losses at the first sign of trouble.
